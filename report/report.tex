\begin{abstract}
Interstellar neutral (ISN) helium reaches 1\,au on two families of Keplerian
orbits: a \emph{direct} beam and an \emph{indirect} beam that wraps around the
Sun before arriving. Because indirect atoms spend longer close to the Sun, the
ratio of the two beam fluxes depends on the ionization rate but not on the
interstellar density or on the absolute detection efficiency. I implement an
analytic hot model of ISN He at 1\,au and use it to predict both beams over
the year as seen by a Sun-pointing spinning detector such as IBEX-Lo or
IMAP-Lo. For a reference ionization rate
$\beta_0 = \SI{1.0e-7}{\per\second}$ at 1\,au, a 43\% higher rate (as
suggested by New Horizons/SWAP pickup ions) lowers the ratio of the late-October
indirect beam to the February direct beam by a factor of 0.61, while a 43\%
higher density leaves it unchanged. This report describes the model, the
assumed observing conditions and the resulting figures, and lists the physics
and instrument effects that still need to be included.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The Sun moves through the partially ionized local interstellar medium, and its
neutral component flows freely into the heliosphere. Helium is the best-studied
ISN species: it has a high ionization potential, it is only weakly filtered at
the heliospheric boundary, and it is too heavy for radiation pressure to matter
much, so its trajectories are close to Keplerian hyperbolae. The flow was
measured directly by the GAS instrument on Ulysses \citep{Witte2004} and, since
2009, by IBEX-Lo on the Interstellar Boundary Explorer
\citep{McComas2009,Fuselier2009,Mobius2009}. Analyses of the IBEX-Lo data
\citep{Bzowski2012,Lee2012} led to a consensus set of inflow parameters
(speed, direction and temperature; \citealt{McComas2015}). IBEX also revealed a
slower, warmer secondary population, the ``Warm Breeze'' \citep{Kubiak2014}.

The main loss process for ISN He inside the heliosphere is ionization, mostly
by solar EUV photons with a contribution from electron impact
\citep{Sokol2019}. The ionization rate controls how much helium survives to
1\,au and therefore enters every interpretation of the data. There are now two
independent hints that the rate is higher than solar-proxy models predict.
\citet{Swaczyna2022} found that the evolution of the ISN He flux seen by IBEX-Lo
over a solar cycle requires either a change in detection efficiency, a higher
ionization rate, or an additional signal source. \citet{Antonik2025} derived
the ionization rate from He$^+$ pickup ions measured by New Horizons/SWAP and
found it to be $\approx$43\% higher than the model rates.

The difficulty is that neither measurement can separate the ionization rate
from the interstellar density on its own. Pickup-ion production scales as
$\nisn\,\beta$, and the direct-beam flux at IBEX-Lo scales as
$\nisn\,\varepsilon\,\exp(-\beta_0\langle X\rangle)$, where $\varepsilon$ is the
poorly known absolute efficiency and $\langle X\rangle$ is an exposure factor
defined below. ``More ionization'', ``more helium'' and ``a different
efficiency'' are therefore partly degenerate. \citet{Swaczyna2025} adopt an ISN
He density of $\approx\SI{0.01}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$ at 1\,au during solar
minimum, which is one of the comparison points used below.

\citet{Bzowski2023} proposed a calibration-free way to measure the ionization
rate: compare the fluxes of the direct and indirect beams. Atoms on indirect
orbits pass around the Sun before reaching 1\,au and accumulate more ionization
exposure, so the flux ratio depends on $\beta_0$ while the density and the
absolute efficiency cancel. An earlier attempt with Ulysses data, summarized by
\citet{Bzowski2023}, was limited because the indirect beam could not be
separated from the background. The pivot platform of IMAP-Lo on the
Interstellar Mapping and Acceleration Probe \citep{McComas2018} makes the
indirect beam observable, and \citet{Kubiak2023} designed a pivot-angle
schedule that includes calibration-free determination of the He and Ne
ionization rates.

This project is a lightweight, independent implementation of the
direct/indirect-beam idea. It asks a narrow question: if the SWAP result is
correct, how large is the change in the beam ratio, and does the ratio really
distinguish a higher ionization rate from a higher density?

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Interstellar gas at infinity}

Far from the Sun the ISN He is a drifting Maxwellian,
\begin{equation}
  f_\mathrm{LISM}(\mathbf{v}) = \frac{\nisn}{\pi^{3/2} v_\mathrm{th}^3}
  \exp\!\left(-\frac{|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}|^2}{v_\mathrm{th}^2}\right),
  \qquad v_\mathrm{th} = \sqrt{2k_BT/m_\mathrm{He}},
\end{equation}
with the IBEX consensus parameters of \citet{McComas2015}: inflow speed
$u = \SI{25.4}{\kilo\metre\per\second}$, upwind ecliptic longitude
$\lambda = \ang{255.7}$, latitude $\beta = \ang{5.1}$ and temperature
$T = \SI{7500}{\kelvin}$ ($v_\mathrm{th}\approx\SI{5.6}{\kilo\metre\per\second}$).
The density at infinity is $\nisn = \SI{0.015}{\per\cubic\centi\metre}$.

\subsection{Keplerian orbits and the two beams}

Radiation pressure is neglected, so each atom moves on a hyperbola under solar
gravity alone (see Section~\ref{sec:limits} for the consequences). For a
phase-space point $(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{v})$ the orbit is fixed by the specific
energy $E = v^2/2 - GM_\odot/r > 0$ and the specific angular momentum
$h = |\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{v}|$. The eccentricity is
$e = \sqrt{1 + 2Eh^2/(GM_\odot)^2}$ and the incoming asymptote lies at true
anomaly $-\nu_\infty$ with $\nu_\infty = \arccos(-1/e)$. The angle the atom has
swept since infinity is $\Delta\nu = \nu + \nu_\infty$, where $\nu$ is its
current true anomaly. Two hyperbolae with the same speed at infinity pass
through every point near the Sun:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \emph{direct} orbit, with $\Delta\nu < \ang{180}$;
  \item the \emph{indirect} orbit, which passes around the Sun first and has
        $\Delta\nu > \ang{180}$.
\end{itemize}
The speed at infinity is $v_\infty = \sqrt{2E}$, and its direction follows from
rotating the position along the orbit back to the asymptote. All of this is
done analytically, without integrating the equations of motion.

\subsection{Ionization exposure}

The ionization rate is assumed to fall off as
$\beta(r) = \beta_0\,(r_0/r)^2$ with $r_0 = 1\,\au$. Along a Keplerian orbit
the true anomaly changes as $\mathrm{d}\nu/\mathrm{d}t = h/r^2$, so the
integrated loss has a closed form:
\begin{equation}
  \int_{-\infty}^{t} \beta\,\mathrm{d}t'
  = \beta_0 r_0^2 \int \frac{\mathrm{d}t'}{r^2}
  = \beta_0\,\frac{r_0^2\,\Delta\nu}{h} \equiv \beta_0 X .
  \label{eq:exposure}
\end{equation}
The survival probability of an atom is $\exp(-\beta_0 X)$. Indirect atoms have
both a larger $\Delta\nu$ and, typically, a smaller $h$, so their exposure $X$
is several times larger than that of direct atoms reaching the same point.
Equation~(\ref{eq:exposure}) is exact for a pure $r^{-2}$ rate on a Keplerian
orbit, and the test suite checks it against numerical orbit integration.

\subsection{Distribution function and moments}

By Liouville's theorem the distribution function is conserved along orbits,
apart from the ionization losses, so at any point inside the heliosphere
\begin{equation}
  f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{v}) =
  f_\mathrm{LISM}\big(\mathbf{v}_\infty(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{v})\big)\,
  \exp\!\big(-\beta_0 X(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{v})\big).
\end{equation}
This is the classical ``hot model'' of interstellar gas in the heliosphere
\citep{Thomas1978,Lee2012}. The density is $n = \int f\,\mathrm{d}^3v$. For an
observer moving with velocity $\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{obs}$, an atom arriving from
direction $\hat{\mathbf{d}}$ with speed $s$ relative to the observer has
heliocentric velocity $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_\mathrm{obs} - s\hat{\mathbf{d}}$,
and the sky-integrated number flux is
\begin{equation}
  F = \int\!\!\int f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{obs}-s\hat{\mathbf{d}})\,
      s^3\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}\Omega .
\end{equation}
Restricting the integral to $\Delta\nu<\ang{180}$ or $\Delta\nu>\ang{180}$ gives
$\Fdir$ and $\Find$.

The integrals are evaluated with the midpoint rule on a grid of arrival
directions (\ang{2} pixels) times speeds (\SI{1}{\kilo\metre\per\second} steps up
to \SI{120}{\kilo\metre\per\second}); the sky maps use a \ang{1} and
\SI{0.5}{\kilo\metre\per\second} grid. Cells with
$|\mathbf{v}_\infty-\mathbf{u}|^2/v_\mathrm{th}^2 > 30$ are dropped. Each
remaining cell is stored once together with its $X$, $\Delta\nu$ and energy, so
any $\beta_0$, density or energy threshold can be applied afterwards without
recomputing the orbits. Halving and quartering the grid step changes the beam
fluxes by less than $10^{-4}$ in most seasons and by about $10^{-3}$ where the
beams overlap.

\subsection{Why the ratio measures the ionization rate}

Each beam is a sum of exponentials in $\beta_0$, so its flux-weighted mean
exposure is
\begin{equation}
  \langle X\rangle = -\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln F}{\mathrm{d}\beta_0}.
\end{equation}
To first order in $\beta_0$,
\begin{equation}
  \ln\frac{\Find}{\Fdir} = \mathrm{const}
     - \beta_0\big(\langle X\rangle_\mathrm{ind}-\langle X\rangle_\mathrm{dir}\big).
  \label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
Both fluxes are proportional to $\nisn$ and, for the same instrument, to the
efficiency $\varepsilon$, so both cancel in the ratio. A change of $\beta_0$
moves the ratio, while a change of $\nisn$ does not. The cancellation of
$\varepsilon$ is exact only if the efficiency is the same for both beams; for
beams arriving at different energies an energy-dependent efficiency leaves a
residual (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Instrumentation}
\label{sec:obs}

\paragraph{Observation.} The observer is on a circular orbit at 1\,au in the
ecliptic plane, moving prograde at \SI{29.78}{\kilo\metre\per\second}. Its
position is given by its ecliptic longitude; the conversion to calendar date
uses a low-precision solar ephemeris (Earth at \ang{30} in late October,
\ang{130} in early February). The annual tables are computed every \ang{2.5}
of longitude, and on a grid of $\beta_0$ from 0 to
\SI{3e-7}{\per\second} in steps of \SI{5e-9}{\per\second}, with
interpolation in $\ln F$ between grid points.

\paragraph{Detector geometry.} IBEX is a Sun-pointing spinning spacecraft, and
IBEX-Lo looks perpendicular to the spin axis, so in each orbit it samples a
great circle \ang{90} from the Sun \citep{McComas2009,Fuselier2009}. This
geometry sees the direct He beam well around February, which is where most
IBEX ISN He analyses come from \citep{Mobius2009,Bzowski2012}. IMAP-Lo is
mounted on a pivot platform that changes the angle between the boresight and
the spin axis \citep{McComas2018}, so it can scan circles of other radii around
the Sun, including ones that cross the indirect beam \citep{Kubiak2023}. In the
model a ``scan circle'' is the set of directions at a given angle from the Sun
direction, and the angle needed to see a beam is the angle between its
flux-weighted centroid and the Sun.

\paragraph{Detection.} IBEX-Lo and IMAP-Lo detect He indirectly, through
negative ions sputtered from a conversion surface, so the efficiency depends
on energy and falls off at low energy. The model mostly uses sky-integrated
fluxes without an instrument response. The energy dependence is represented
only by a sharp lower threshold of 0, 20 or \SI{40}{\electronvolt} in the
observer frame.

\paragraph{Reference observations.} The direct beam is taken at its February
peak (Earth at \ang{130}), where IBEX-Lo has measured it for over a decade. The
indirect beam is taken in late October (Earth at \ang{30}), where it is bright,
well separated from the direct beam and about \ang{59} from the Sun. The two
competing explanations of the SWAP result are
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1}: the ionization rate is $1.43\times$ the model value,
        $\beta_0 = \SI{1.43e-7}{\per\second}$, at fixed density;
  \item \textbf{H2}: the density is $1.43\times$ higher at the model rate
        $\beta_0 = \SI{1.0e-7}{\per\second}$, giving the same pickup-ion
        production $\nisn\beta$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Density at 1\,au (Figure~\ref{fig:dens}).} This is a consistency

\paragraph{The beams over the year (Figure~\ref{fig:annual}).} The direct beam
is bright all year, peaking at $\approx\SI{2.1e5}{\per\square\centi\metre\per\second}$
when Earth passes through the focusing cone. The indirect beam is comparable to
the direct beam from November to December. It becomes negligible
around the upwind season (May to July), when Earth is far from the region where
indirect orbits arrive. Between Earth longitudes of about \ang{60} and \ang{90}
the beams overlap on the sky and cannot be separated. The indirect beam is
more sensitive to $\beta_0$ throughout. Its energy in autumn is
\SIrange{100}{110}{\electronvolt}, compared with $\approx\SI{129}{\electronvolt}$
for the February direct beam. The two reference observations therefore differ
by about 20\% in energy, so an energy-dependent efficiency cancels only partly
in their ratio. \citet{Bzowski2023} recommend choosing pairs of observations at
matched energies for this reason. Panel (c) shows that the indirect beam stays
\ang{50}--\ang{60} from the Sun in autumn, so it needs a pivot angle smaller
than \ang{90}.

\paragraph{Exposure and response (Figure~\ref{fig:resp}).} The indirect beam
accumulates 1.5--5 times more exposure than the direct beam observed at the
same time, with the largest contrast early in autumn: the ratio
$\langle X\rangle_\mathrm{ind}/\langle X\rangle_\mathrm{dir}$ is 4.7 at Earth
longitude \ang{0}, 2.6 at \ang{30} and 1.5 at \ang{55}. For the reference pair,
$\langle X\rangle$ is \SI{0.67e7}{\second} for the February direct beam and
\SI{1.83e7}{\second} for the October indirect beam. Under H1 the direct beam
drops to 0.75 and the indirect beam to 0.46 of the model value, so the ratio
drops to 0.61. Under H2 both beams rise by the same factor of 1.43 and the
ratio does not change. The absolute direct-beam flux could separate H1 and H2
only if the absolute efficiency were known to much better than the 25--43\%
difference between them; the ratio does not need it.

\paragraph{Breaking the degeneracy (Figure~\ref{fig:degen}).} The pseudo-data
assume $10^4$ counts in the February direct beam, and the October indirect-beam
counts ($\approx750$) follow from the same exposure time and efficiency. A
single direct-beam measurement constrains only a curved band in the
$(\beta_0, \nisn\varepsilon)$ plane: a higher ionization rate can be
compensated by more helium or a higher efficiency. Adding the indirect beam
closes the contour around the true point. H2, with its best-fitting
$\nisn\varepsilon$, lies at $\Delta\chi^2\approx199$ from the truth. This is
an idealized illustration of how the second beam lifts the degeneracy, not a
forecast of the achievable precision, which depends on the effects in
Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\paragraph{Energy threshold (Figure~\ref{fig:emin}).} At Earth longitude
\ang{30} the indirect beam (mean energy $\approx\SI{106}{\electronvolt}$) is
unaffected by a threshold of up to \SI{40}{\electronvolt}. The slow direct
population seen in autumn ($\approx\SIrange{10}{20}{\electronvolt}$) drops from
\num{2.8e4} to \SI{8.3e2}{\per\square\centi\metre\per\second} for
$E>\SI{20}{\electronvolt}$, and to $\approx\SI{7}{\per\square\centi\metre\per\second}$ for
$E>\SI{40}{\electronvolt}$. A realistic low-energy efficiency cut-off therefore
helps by suppressing the slow direct atoms that share the sky with the indirect
beam in autumn. The February direct beam is unaffected by these thresholds.

\section{Limitations and future prospects}
\label{sec:limits}

The model is a feasibility study of the size of the beam-ratio signal. A full
analysis of IMAP-Lo data would need several additions, listed roughly in order
of importance. Some of them are already being worked on.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Radiation pressure.} The orbits are purely Keplerian.
  \citet{KowalskaLeszczynska2023} showed that radiation pressure on He changes
  the IMAP-Lo fluxes by up to $\sim$9\% for the indirect beam, likely more than
  the statistical uncertainties. Including it breaks the closed form of
  Equation~(\ref{eq:exposure}); the natural upgrade is numerical orbits with a
  radiation-pressure factor.

  \item \textbf{Other near-Sun losses.} The beam ratio measures the
  \emph{total} extra loss of indirect atoms, not photoionization alone. Any
  process that removes atoms preferentially close to the Sun lowers the ratio
  in the same way as a higher $\beta_0$. Elastic scattering of ISN He by
  solar-wind particles has been proposed as an alternative to a $\sim$40\%
  higher ionization rate \citep{ElasticScattering2024}. Separating the two
  would rely on their different energy and angular signatures.

  \item \textbf{Secondary helium.} The Warm Breeze \citep{Kubiak2014} arrives
  from a different direction with its own direct and indirect beams, and its
  contribution to the autumn indirect beam has to be modelled. The code
  supports this by computing a second population with different inflow
  parameters and adding the fluxes.

  \item \textbf{Time-dependent and non-$r^{-2}$ ionization.} Indirect atoms
  spend months near the Sun, and the two beams are observed months apart, so
  solar-cycle variations of the ionization rate matter. Electron-impact
  ionization falls off faster than $r^{-2}$ and is not described by a single
  $\beta_0$ \citep{Sokol2019}. Both effects break the closed form; a numerical
  exposure integral along each orbit is the planned upgrade.

  \item \textbf{Instrument response and energy matching.} The model uses
  sky-integrated fluxes and a sharp energy threshold. It does not include the
  IMAP-Lo collimator, the pivot schedule of \citet{Kubiak2023}, spin-axis
  offsets, the energy-dependent sputtering efficiency or the duty cycle.
  Choosing direct/indirect pairs at matched energies \citep{Bzowski2023} would
  reduce the efficiency residual.

  \item \textbf{Inflow-parameter systematics and counting statistics.} The
  uncertainties of the inflow speed, direction and temperature
  \citep{McComas2015} also move the beam ratio, and the number of counts needed
  to detect the signal depends on the observing season. A first estimate was
  removed from this version and is being redone. Because the inflow parameters
  are correlated and are themselves derived from direct-beam data, the
  consistent approach is a joint fit of the inflow parameters and $\beta_0$ to
  both beams.

  \item \textbf{Geometry.} IMAP's position near L1 (0.99\,au), the eccentricity
  of Earth's orbit and the filtration of He in the outer heliosheath are
  ignored. These are expected to be small corrections.
\end{enumerate}
